# A Job Is a Bundle of Tasks

## Idea
AI does not take jobs, it takes tasks, and a job survives in proportion to how much of its bundle is left once one task gets cheap.

## Definition
"AI will replace this job" usually means "AI can do one of the things this job does", and those are very different sentences. A job is a bundle: a radiologist reads scans, but also chooses which scan a patient needs, talks to the surgeon, does image-guided procedures, and makes the call when the picture is unclear. When AI gets good at one task in the bundle, that task gets cheap, and nobody pays a premium for it any more, including your employer. What you get paid for is the rest of the bundle: the judgment, the context, the calls nobody else in the room can make. The real danger is the thin bundle, a job that is mostly one task. If that task is the one AI got good at, there is little left to hold the role together. So the useful question is not "will AI take my job" but "what is my work worth now that some of it is cheap, and what do I add to the bundle next?" The exercise: list every task you do in a week, mark the ones AI already does well, and see what is left.

## Source
Captured from my own long-form video on AI and job fear (October 2026).

The task-based view of work comes from David Autor and Daron Acemoglu's task framework (Acemoglu & Autor, 2011), which models automation as taking tasks rather than whole occupations. The "weak bundle / strong bundle" language comes from Luis Garicano, Jin Li and Yanhui Wu, *Weak Bundle, Strong Bundle: How AI Redraws Job Boundaries* (CEPR Discussion Paper DP21453, 2026). They argue that what decides AI's effect is how costly it is to split the AI-doable tasks off from the rest. Weak bundles get unbundled and partly automated; strong bundles keep the human and protect workers' share of revenue.

The canonical case is Geoffrey Hinton's 2016 remark in Toronto that it was "completely obvious" AI would out-read radiologists within five to ten years, so we should stop training them. He later said he had spoken too broadly and meant image analysis only. In 2025 the average US radiologist earned about $571K (Medscape, reported by Fortune in May 2026), with thousands of unfilled openings. Klarna is the second case. In February 2024 its assistant handled 2.3 million chats in a month, the work of about 700 agents. By May 2025 its CEO admitted cost had been "a too predominant evaluation factor", quality had dropped, and it began hiring humans again. *(The capture's "10% more radiologists than ten years ago" could not be verified and is left out.)*

## Compass

**Roots** — *where this comes from*
This is the economics underneath [[Tie Your Identity to How You Think]]: a role anchored to one executable task is a thin bundle, and a role anchored to judgment is a strong one. It also explains why [[Depth Is Cheap]] does not mean people are cheap, because the depth was only ever one task in the bundle.

**Paths** — *where this leads*
Once a task gets cheap, the remaining value moves to [[Direct and Check]], telling the tool what to do and catching it when it is wrong. That gives the bundle a growth strategy instead of a defensive one, which is the instinct behind [[Learning Agility]].

**Neighbors** — *what lives nearby*
It rhymes with [[Only the Rich Will Burn Candles]]: when a technology takes over a task, the task does not vanish, it re-prices. The bundle view says the same of the work around it, which gets more valuable rather than less.

**Clash** — *what pushes against this*
Bundles are not fixed. A company can redesign the role so the cheap task is split off and the rest is handed to fewer, more senior people, which is exactly the entry-level squeeze in [[Ship Reps, Not Grunt Work]]. The bundle argument protects the job category better than it protects any one person in it.
