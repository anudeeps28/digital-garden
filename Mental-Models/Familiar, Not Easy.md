# Familiar, Not Easy

## Idea
There is no such thing as easy or hard — only familiar or unfamiliar.

## Definition
The things you're "naturally good at" aren't natural — you just got a head start you don't remember getting. Family, school, childhood sports, a great teacher in third grade — all of it shaped what feels effortless today. Everything that feels impossible right now simply hasn't been practiced enough. The rule: you're not allowed to call something hard until you've tried it a hundred times. Before that threshold, you're not struggling with difficulty — you're struggling with unfamiliarity. Everything is a skill issue.

## Source
Anudeep Sharma's content (Instagram, August 2026). The underlying idea echoes Anders Ericsson's deliberate practice research and the growth mindset work of Carol Dweck ("Mindset," 2006), but the specific reframe of "familiar vs unfamiliar" rather than "easy vs hard" is the distinctive contribution.

## Compass

**Roots** — This is a direct descendant of [[The Parable of the Pottery Class]] and [[Volume Negates Luck]] — both argue that quantity of attempts, not innate talent, produces quality.

**Paths** — Internalizing this reframe leads to [[We Learn by Doing, Not by Watching]] and removes the psychological barrier that keeps people in tutorial hell instead of building real projects.

**Neighbors** — This lives near [[The Winner Has Failed More Than the Loser Has Tried]], sharing the same emphasis on reps over talent, and [[85% Rule]], which calibrates the right level of unfamiliarity for optimal learning.

**Clash** — This can become toxic if taken too literally — some things genuinely require prerequisites (you can't brute-force quantum physics with zero math), and [[Errare Humanum Est]] reminds us that acknowledging real difficulty isn't weakness.
