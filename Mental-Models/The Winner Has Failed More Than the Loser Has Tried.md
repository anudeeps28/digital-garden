# The Winner Has Failed More Than the Loser Has Tried

## Idea
The gap between winners and losers isn't talent or luck — it's that winners have accumulated more failures, which means they've accumulated more attempts.

## Definition
This reframes failure from a negative outcome to a counting metric — if you've failed more times than someone else has even tried, you're ahead by definition. The math is simple: attempts multiplied by success rate equals successes. Most people optimize for success rate (avoiding failure), but the winners optimize for attempts (maximizing exposure to opportunity). Elon Musk's rockets blew up publicly and repeatedly before SpaceX landed one. The pattern isn't that he succeeded despite the failures — it's that the failures were the prerequisite for the successes. Every public failure was a rep that the people criticizing from the sidelines never took.

## Source
Common aphorism; illustrated by Elon Musk's trajectory from serial public failures to building SpaceX and Tesla.

## Compass

**Roots** — *where this comes from*
This is [[Volume Negates Luck]] applied specifically to failure — if you do enough reps, some will fail, but enough will succeed that the failures become irrelevant. It's also a descendant of [[The Flywheel Concept]], where the early failures are just the cost of getting the wheel spinning.

**Paths** — *where this leads*
Internalizing this leads to a fundamental mindset shift — from "how do I avoid failure?" to "how do I increase my attempt rate?" This reframes [[Consistency]] from a discipline practice into a failure-accumulation strategy, which sounds worse but is actually more honest.

**Neighbors** — *what lives nearby*
[[The Parable of the Pottery Class]] is the most direct neighbor — the students who made more pots (and therefore more bad pots) ended up with the best pots. [[Errare Humanum Est]] captures the philosophical acceptance that error is intrinsic to being human, which is a gentler version of the same truth.

**Clash** — *what pushes against this*
[[Acceptance]] and [[Calm and Peace]] push against the relentless attempt-maximization framing — sometimes the wise move is to stop trying and accept the current state. There's also survivorship bias: we only see the winners who failed a lot; we don't see the people who failed a lot and never recovered.
