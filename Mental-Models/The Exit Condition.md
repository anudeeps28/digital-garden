# The Exit Condition

## Idea
AI removed the natural brake from your work — the cost of "one more pass" used to be your evening; now it's a thirty-second prompt — so you must write the exit condition before you start.

## Definition
A loop with no exit condition is a bug you'd never ship in code, yet it's exactly the workflow most people run with AI every day. Perfectionism used to have a built-in brake: your own time. AI removed that brake. Now every iteration feels like progress because something new appears on screen, but you're orbiting, not advancing. The fix has three parts, written down *before* you touch the prompt: (1) a **time-box** — how long does this task get, as a number, not "until it feels done"; (2) **acceptance criteria** — what does "done" actually mean, minimally and specifically; (3) an **edge-case list** — decided upfront, because the AI will happily chase every rabbit hole without ever asking why. This isn't productivity advice. This is engineering. The design, the architecture, the decision about what "done" means — that was always the actual job.

## Source
Anudeep Sharma's content (YouTube, August 2026). The concept adapts the computer science principle of loop termination to personal workflow, influenced by agile development's "definition of done" and the engineering practice of acceptance criteria.

## Compass

**Roots** — This grows from software engineering's [[Define Contract Before Implementation]] principle and the broader idea that [[Discipline Over Motivation]] requires structure, not willpower.

**Paths** — Applying exit conditions leads to [[Slow Productivity]] thinking — doing fewer things but finishing them properly — and prevents the new form of burnout that comes from infinite AI-assisted orbiting.

**Neighbors** — This sits beside [[The Daily Highlight]] (picking one thing that matters) and [[The Eisenhower Matrix]] (deciding what's worth doing at all), both of which help define what deserves your time before you start.

**Clash** — [[Full Effort, Loose Grip]] pushes back slightly — sometimes exploration without a strict exit condition produces unexpected breakthroughs, and over-defining "done" can kill serendipity in creative work.
