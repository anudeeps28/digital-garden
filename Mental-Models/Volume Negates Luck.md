# Volume Negates Luck

## Idea
Do enough reps and luck becomes irrelevant — volume turns probability from an obstacle into a guarantee.

## Definition
When you attempt something once, the outcome depends heavily on chance. But when you attempt it a hundred or a thousand times, the law of large numbers kicks in and your average outcome converges toward the expected value. In practice: one cold email might get ignored, but a thousand sent with decent copy will land meetings. One video might flop, but a hundred videos will surface your best ideas. Volume doesn't just increase your odds — it removes luck from the equation entirely by making success a statistical inevitability.

## Source
Popularized by Alex Hormozi in his content and business philosophy; rooted in the law of large numbers from probability theory.

## Compass

**Roots** — *where this comes from*
This is a street-level articulation of the law of large numbers applied to effort and output, and it sits squarely under the umbrella of [[Consistency]] — the idea that showing up repeatedly is more powerful than any single brilliant attempt.

**Paths** — *where this leads*
Volume negating luck is the mathematical backbone of [[The Flywheel Concept]] — the early reps feel pointless, but they're loading the flywheel. It also connects to [[The Parable of the Pottery Class]], where the students who made more pots made better pots, because quantity is the precondition for quality.

**Neighbors** — *what lives nearby*
[[Consistency Over Intensity]] is the same truth from a sustainability angle — it's not about one heroic sprint, it's about enough reps to let the numbers work. The [[85% Rule]] complements this by suggesting the optimal intensity for those reps: not maximum effort, but sustainable effort at high volume.

**Clash** — *what pushes against this*
[[Pareto Principle]] pushes back — not all reps are equal, and volume without targeting the right 20% is just busy work. The nuance is that volume negates luck only when each rep has a nonzero probability of success; mindless repetition of a broken process just produces a thousand failures.
