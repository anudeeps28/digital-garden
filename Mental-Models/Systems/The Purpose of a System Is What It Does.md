# The Purpose of a System Is What It Does

## Idea
Judge a system's real purpose by what it consistently produces, not by its mission statement.

## Definition
Meadows says a system's purpose is deduced from behavior, not from rhetoric or stated goals. A government that says it values the environment but spends almost nothing protecting it does not, in practice, have environmental protection as a purpose. A company that says "customers first" while rewarding staff only for quarterly revenue has revenue as its purpose. Stated goals are claims. Persistent outcomes are evidence. The gap between them is not always hypocrisy. It usually comes from incentives, measures and feedback loops that quietly point somewhere other than the mission. To learn what a system is for, watch what it keeps doing and what it rewards.

## Source
Donella H. Meadows, *Thinking in Systems*, Chapter 1. The phrase "the purpose of a system is what it does" (POSIWID) comes from cybernetician Stafford Beer, who used it from the 1970s and wrote it down in a 2001 address. See [[Thinking in Systems]].

## Compass

**Roots** — *where this comes from*
It is the "purpose" third of [[Elements, Interconnections, and Purpose]], made into a test you can apply. Like [[Consensus Is Not Evidence]], it tells you to trust observed behavior over repeated claims.

**Paths** — *where this leads*
When the actual purpose drifts from the intended one, you get [[Seeking the Wrong Goal]], and fixing it means working at the "goals" rung of [[Leverage Points]].

**Neighbors** — *what lives nearby*
[[Report the Outcome, Not the Metric]] applies the same instinct to your own work: say what actually changed, not what you aimed at or what a proxy score claims.

**Clash** — *what pushes against this*
Used carelessly, it turns every side effect into intent, as if every outcome a system produces were deliberately chosen. Some outcomes are emergent accidents, and [[Bounded Rationality]] explains how well-meaning actors produce results nobody wanted.
