# Every Model Is a Simplification

## Idea
Everything you think you know about a system is a model, and every model leaves things out. Systems surprise us in exactly the parts our models left out.

## Definition
Meadows writes that everything we think we know about the world is a model: our mental maps, spreadsheets, diagrams and theories. Models are necessary because reality is too interconnected to hold in your head, and many are useful. But every model draws a boundary, chooses which variables count, and assumes how they connect. Surprises come from what lies outside the boundary, from connections the model treated as fixed, and from stocks it ignored. The right stance is neither trust nor cynicism. Hold models loosely, state their assumptions, and expect the world to disagree.

## Source
Donella H. Meadows, *Thinking in Systems*, Chapter 4. Statistician George Box put it more famously: "All models are wrong, but some are useful" (1976). Alfred Korzybski's "the map is not the territory" (1931) is the older version. See [[Thinking in Systems]].

## Compass

**Roots** — *where this comes from*
It is the epistemic warning attached to all of [[Systems Thinking]], including its own diagrams. [[Critical Thinking Skills]] starts from the same humility about premises.

**Paths** — *where this leads*
Because models are partial, Meadows' advice in [[Dancing with Systems]] begins with exposing your mental models so others can find the gaps. The [[Assumption Register]] and [[Prediction Journal]] are concrete ways to do that.

**Neighbors** — *what lives nearby*
[[A Stale Source Is Confidently Wrong]] is a model that was once accurate and has since expired. [[Tag Claims as Cited or Assumed]] makes visible which parts of a model rest on evidence and which rest on guesses.

**Clash** — *what pushes against this*
Taken too far it becomes paralysis: if every model is wrong, why commit to any? The answer is [[First Make It Work, Then Make It Better]]: act on the best model you have and let feedback correct it.
