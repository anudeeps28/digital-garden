# Balancing Feedback Loop

## Idea
A balancing loop pushes a stock back toward a target: the further it drifts, the harder the system pushes back.

## Definition
A balancing (negative) feedback loop compares a stock to a desired level and adjusts a flow to close the gap. A thermostat turns the heat on when the room is too cold. A personal budget makes you cut spending when the account runs low. Your body sweats when it is hot. Balancing loops are what make systems stable and goal-seeking, and they resist change in either direction. Two practical lessons follow. If something will not move however hard you push, look for the balancing loop pushing back. And if you want stability, protect the balancing loops that provide it.

## Source
Donella H. Meadows, *Thinking in Systems*, Chapter 1. The idea comes from control theory and Norbert Wiener's *Cybernetics* (1948). See [[Thinking in Systems]].

## Compass

**Roots** — *where this comes from*
It is one of the two kinds of feedback in [[Systems Thinking]], always acting on some stock from [[Stocks and Flows]]. Engineers formalized it as the [[PID Controller]], which corrects based on the size, history and rate of change of the error.

**Paths** — *where this leads*
When the correction arrives late, a balancing loop overshoots and swings back and forth ([[Delays Cause Oscillation]]). When many actors' balancing loops pull toward different goals, you get [[Policy Resistance]].

**Neighbors** — *what lives nearby*
[[Hedonic Adaptation]] is a balancing loop in the mind, pulling happiness back to a set point after any change. [[Backpressure]] and [[Rate Limiting]] are deliberately designed balancing loops that keep software systems inside safe limits.

**Clash** — *what pushes against this*
Its opposite is the [[Reinforcing Feedback Loop]], which amplifies change instead of resisting it. A balancing loop is also only as good as its target: if the target is wrong or slowly sinking, it faithfully steers toward the wrong place, which is [[Drift to Low Performance]].
