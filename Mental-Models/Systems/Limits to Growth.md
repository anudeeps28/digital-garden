# Limits to Growth

## Idea
No physical system grows forever. Every reinforcing loop eventually runs into a constraint, and the system either levels off, oscillates, or overshoots and collapses.

## Definition
Growth is driven by a reinforcing loop, but it always draws on something finite: food, energy, money, attention, customers. As the system grows, two economic forces close in. **Increasing marginal cost**: each additional unit of growth costs more than the last, because the easy resources are used first. **Diminishing marginal benefit**: each additional unit of input yields less than the last. Where growth ends depends on the constraint. If the limit pushes back early and gradually, growth slows into an S-curve. If the limit is depleted or reacts late, the system overshoots and collapses. Meadows' "systems zoo" catalogs these shapes: equilibrium, growth then collapse, and oscillation. Planning for growth means asking what the limit is and how fast it pushes back.

## Source
Donella H. Meadows, *Thinking in Systems*, Chapter 2. Built on Meadows, Meadows, Randers & Behrens, *The Limits to Growth* (Club of Rome, 1972), which modeled exactly these dynamics for the world economy. Diminishing returns goes back to Turgot and Ricardo. See [[Thinking in Systems]].

## Compass

**Roots** — *where this comes from*
It is what happens when a [[Reinforcing Feedback Loop]] meets a [[Balancing Feedback Loop]] that has been waiting at the edge of the system all along.

**Paths** — *where this leads*
The practical question is which [[Limiting Factor]] binds, and whether the resource is [[Stock-Limited vs Flow-Limited Resources|stock-limited or flow-limited]]. When the limit is a shared resource, growth turns into the [[Tragedy of the Commons]].

**Neighbors** — *what lives nearby*
[[Unit Economics]] applies this to a business: model the cost of one unit at today's load and at ten times it, because costs rarely scale linearly. [[Backpressure]] is a software system admitting it has a limit before it collapses into one.

**Clash** — *what pushes against this*
Technology and substitution repeatedly push limits outward, which is the economists' standard rebuttal to the 1972 report. A limit moving, though, is not the same as no limit, and [[Linear Minds in a Nonlinear World]] tend to read a past expansion as proof of infinite room.
