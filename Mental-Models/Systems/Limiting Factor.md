# Limiting Factor

## Idea
At any moment, a system's growth is set by its scarcest necessary input, and as the system grows, that bottleneck moves.

## Definition
A plant with plenty of nitrogen but no phosphorus stops growing, and adding more nitrogen does nothing. Only the scarce input matters. Organizations work the same way. A company hires a strong sales team, demand explodes, and now production is the bottleneck. Fix production, and cash flow or hiring becomes the limit. Two mistakes are common. One is pouring resources into an input that is already plentiful. The other is assuming yesterday's bottleneck is still today's. Growth itself changes which factor is limiting, so you have to keep asking what is scarce right now.

## Source
Donella H. Meadows, *Thinking in Systems*, Chapters 2 and 4. The concept is Liebig's Law of the Minimum, developed by agronomist Carl Sprengel (1828) and popularized by Justus von Liebig. Eliyahu Goldratt's *The Goal* (1984) and Theory of Constraints carry the same idea into operations. See [[Thinking in Systems]].

## Compass

**Roots** — *where this comes from*
It is the specific mechanism behind [[Limits to Growth]]: the general limit always shows up as one particular scarce input.

**Paths** — *where this leads*
Identifying the binding constraint tells you where effort actually pays off, so the limiting factor is a practical shortcut to [[Leverage Points]]. Whether that constraint is a stock or a flow is covered in [[Stock-Limited vs Flow-Limited Resources]].

**Neighbors** — *what lives nearby*
The [[Pareto Principle]] also says most of the outcome depends on a few inputs. [[Kanban]]'s work-in-progress limits make the bottleneck stage visible so a team stops overloading it.

**Clash** — *what pushes against this*
In tightly coupled systems there is rarely one clean limit. Inputs interact, so removing one constraint can make several others bind at once, and the "single bottleneck" story can be too neat.
