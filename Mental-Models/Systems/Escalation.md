# Escalation

## Idea
When each side's goal is to stay a little ahead of the other, every move provokes a bigger countermove and the contest spirals.

## Definition
Escalation is a reinforcing loop between rivals. A raises their stake to beat B, B raises theirs to beat A, and so on: arms races, price wars, political attack ads, loudness wars in music mastering, and email threads that grow more hostile with each reply. Each response looks justified as a reaction to the last provocation, so neither side feels like the aggressor. The stock that drains is usually goodwill, money or trust. Meadows names two exits. The best is to refuse to compete, by unilaterally disarming and accepting being "behind" for a while. Otherwise, negotiate a new system with balancing loops that cap the competition.

## Source
Donella H. Meadows, *Thinking in Systems*, Chapter 5, trap #4. The arms-race model goes back to Lewis Fry Richardson's work in the 1930s–40s. See [[Thinking in Systems]].

## Compass

**Roots** — *where this comes from*
It is a [[Reinforcing Feedback Loop]] that runs through two actors instead of one stock.

**Paths** — *where this leads*
Unchecked, escalation ends in exhaustion, or it settles into [[Success to the Successful]] once one side pulls far enough ahead to dominate. Stepping out of it is a paradigm move, the top rung of [[Leverage Points]].

**Neighbors** — *what lives nearby*
[[Zero-Sum vs Positive-Sum Games]] explains why escalation is miserable: it turns any contest into one where your gain must be their loss. [[There Is Nothing to Prove]] is the personal version of unilateral disarmament.

**Clash** — *what pushes against this*
Unilateral disarmament assumes the other side will not exploit it. Against a truly predatory actor, refusing to compete can just mean losing, so the exit often needs verified, mutual de-escalation rather than goodwill alone.
