# Information Flows Shape Behavior

## Idea
Putting missing feedback where decisions are made often changes behavior more cheaply than any rule or incentive.

## Definition
Many interconnections in a system are flows of information, and many failures come from information that is missing, late or sent to the wrong place. Meadows tells the story of a Dutch housing development where some houses had their electricity meter in the front hallway and others in the basement. With no other difference, the hallway households used about a third less electricity, because the feedback was in front of them every day. Restoring a missing feedback loop is a high-leverage move: it is cheap and works through people's own judgment rather than against it. When people act badly, check first whether they can see the consequences of what they do.

## Source
Donella H. Meadows, *Thinking in Systems*, Chapters 1 and 6. "Information flows" is leverage point #6 in her 1999 essay "Leverage Points: Places to Intervene in a System". She cites the Dutch meter example as a real case, and it is often repeated. Treat the one-third figure as illustrative. See [[Thinking in Systems]].

## Compass

**Roots** — *where this comes from*
It follows from the observation in [[Elements, Interconnections, and Purpose]] that many interconnections are information, so changing who knows what changes the system.

**Paths** — *where this leads*
It sits in the upper half of [[Leverage Points]], above physical structure and parameters. It is also the fix for [[Bounded Rationality]] and for the late signals behind [[Delays Cause Oscillation]].

**Neighbors** — *what lives nearby*
[[Instrument the Hypothesis]] and [[Fail Fast Fail Loudly]] are the engineering versions: surface the signal at the moment and place where someone can act on it. [[Kanban]] works mostly by making invisible queues visible.

**Clash** — *what pushes against this*
Information is not always neutral or welcome. A visible metric invites gaming ([[Seeking the Wrong Goal]], [[Rule Beating]]), and powerful actors often resist transparency because feedback would threaten them.
