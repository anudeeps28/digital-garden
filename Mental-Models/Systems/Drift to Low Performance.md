# Drift to Low Performance

## Idea
When the standard is set by past performance and bad results count for more than good ones, the standard slides down a little at a time and nobody notices.

## Definition
Meadows calls this "eroding goals". A system aims for a goal, but that goal is partly set by how it has been doing lately. A bad stretch lowers the bar ("this is normal for us"), lower expectations mean less corrective effort, performance slips further, and the bar drops again. A negativity bias makes it worse, because we remember failures more vividly than successes. Teams that once had zero flaky tests now tolerate twenty. Fitness routines fade the same way. Meadows' remedy is to anchor standards to absolute benchmarks rather than recent results, and to let your best performances set the bar, not your worst.

## Source
Donella H. Meadows, *Thinking in Systems*, Chapter 5, trap #3. Related to sociologist Diane Vaughan's "normalization of deviance" from her study of the Challenger disaster (1996). See [[Thinking in Systems]].

## Compass

**Roots** — *where this comes from*
It is a [[Balancing Feedback Loop]] whose target is quietly being reset by a slow [[Reinforcing Feedback Loop]] in the wrong direction, and the large buffer of [[Stocks and Flows]] keeps the decline invisible.

**Paths** — *where this leads*
The fix is to change the goal, a high rung on [[Leverage Points]], and to make fixed standards explicit. [[Codify Lessons Into Defaults]] does this by writing the bar into the system so it cannot quietly drift.

**Neighbors** — *what lives nearby*
[[Hedonic Adaptation]] is the same drift in the other direction: good conditions become the new baseline. [[Kaizen]] is the deliberate reverse, a loop that ratchets the standard upward a little at a time.

**Clash** — *what pushes against this*
Anchoring to your best can turn into perfectionism and [[Toxic Productivity]]. Sometimes lowering a standard is the honest response to a real constraint, and the trap is only when it happens without anyone deciding it.
