# Elements, Interconnections, and Purpose

## Idea
Every system has three parts: elements, the interconnections between them, and a purpose. The elements are the most visible part and usually matter least.

## Definition
A system is a set of elements linked so that together they produce a pattern of behavior aimed at some function or purpose. A football team has players (elements), rules, tactics and communication (interconnections), and a purpose: winning, or making money, or entertaining fans. Change every player and it is still recognizably the same team. Change the rules and it becomes a different game. Change the purpose, for example from winning to losing, and everything shifts. So the parts that are easiest to see are usually the least important. Many interconnections are flows of information (prices, signals, rumors) rather than physical links, which is why systems can be far more connected than they look.

## Source
Donella H. Meadows, *Thinking in Systems*, Chapter 1, "The Basics". See [[Thinking in Systems]].

**Roots** — *where this comes from*

**Paths** — *where this leads*
The ranking of parts (purpose above interconnections above elements) reappears almost unchanged as the top and bottom of [[Leverage Points]]. Because purpose is the hardest part to see, it leads directly to [[The Purpose of a System Is What It Does]].

**Neighbors** — *what lives nearby*
[[Information Flows Shape Behavior]] expands the point that many interconnections are information, not physical links. Software architecture works the same way: [[Clean Architecture]] cares more about the boundaries and dependencies between components than about any single component.

**Clash** — *what pushes against this*
Our instincts run the other way. We blame or reward individual elements, such as the CEO or the star player, because they are visible, and that keeps attention away from the rules and goals that actually drive the outcome.
