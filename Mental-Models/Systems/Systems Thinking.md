# Systems Thinking

## Idea
A system's behavior comes from its structure, so lasting problems are fixed by changing that structure, not by blaming events or people.

## Definition
Systems thinking means looking past single events ("sales dropped this quarter") to the patterns behind them ("sales rise and fall every eighteen months"), and then to the structure that produces those patterns: the stocks, flows and feedback loops connecting the parts. Donella Meadows' central claim is that a system largely causes its own behavior. Outside forces trigger change, but the response is set by how the system is wired. If you replace the people and keep the structure, you usually get the same results. The practical habit is to ask "what structure would produce this?" before asking "who did this?"

## Source
Donella H. Meadows, *Thinking in Systems: A Primer* (2008). The field grew out of Jay Forrester's system dynamics work at MIT in the 1950s–60s. Meadows was a student of Forrester's and lead author of *The Limits to Growth* (1972). Peter Senge brought it to management in *The Fifth Discipline* (1990). See [[Thinking in Systems]].

## Compass

**Roots** — *where this comes from*
It is the general version of [[Your Environment Is Your Destiny]]: behavior follows from the setup around it, so the leverage is in the setup. Everything else in the book builds on three primitives: [[Elements, Interconnections, and Purpose]], [[Stocks and Flows]], and feedback loops.

**Paths** — *where this leads*
Once you see behavior as structural, the question becomes where to push, and [[Leverage Points]] ranks the answers. It also gives a vocabulary for recurring failures, starting with [[Policy Resistance]] and [[Shifting the Burden]].

**Neighbors** — *what lives nearby*
[[Environment Design]] is systems thinking applied to one person's habits: change the structure of the room and the behavior changes without any extra willpower. [[Critical Thinking Skills]] asks you to question the premise, and systems thinking asks you to question the frame.

**Clash** — *what pushes against this*
Taken too far it becomes fatalism: "it's the system" can excuse individual responsibility. Meadows is clear that [[Every Model Is a Simplification]], so a systems diagram is a hypothesis to test, not a verdict.
