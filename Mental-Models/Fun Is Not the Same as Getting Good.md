# Fun Is Not the Same as Getting Good

## Idea
Enjoying an activity and improving at it are different states, and the study that feels best is usually not the study that makes you better.

## Definition
The trap is subtle because it never feels like procrastination. You are working hard, on the real subject, and genuinely enjoying it — and improving far slower than someone doing less pleasant work. The pleasant version tends to be the part you are already good at, or the part with no feedback attached: reading another chapter, deriving something elegant, watching a lecture. Improvement lives where the difficulty exceeds your current reach and something tells you immediately that you got it wrong. The test is not whether you enjoyed the hour. It is whether anything in it could have told you that you were wrong.

## Source
Captured from the "how to learn machine learning" video (September 2026) — I went deep on calculus for fun, loved every month of it, and it was the single thing that cost me the most time. See the [[Machine Learning Roadmap]] for the full path that conclusion produced.

Anders Ericsson's research on deliberate practice makes the same split formally: deliberate practice is "not inherently enjoyable" and is undertaken instrumentally to improve. He argued it is actively incompatible with flow — flow occurs when challenge and skill are balanced and feels effortless, while deliberate practice requires challenge beyond current skill, explicit goals and constant error correction. Experts seek flow in performance; they do not improve during it.

## Compass

**Roots** — *where this comes from*
This is the uncomfortable corollary of [[We Learn by Doing, Not by Watching]] — not all doing counts, and the doing that counts is the kind with a verdict attached. It needs the machinery of [[Discipline Over Motivation]], since the right work will rarely be the work you feel like doing.

**Paths** — *where this leads*
The practical answer is to organise learning around a feedback cycle rather than a syllabus, which is the whole argument of [[Learn the Loop, Not the Algorithm]], and to pick up theory only when it blocks something concrete. It reframes difficulty as information, the way [[Familiar, Not Easy]] does.

**Neighbors** — *what lives nearby*
It sits close to [[Consistency Over Intensity]] — both reject the idea that how a session feels is evidence of what it produced. It shares a mechanism with [[85% Rule]], which likewise separates subjective exertion from actual output.

**Clash** — *what pushes against this*
[[You Cannot Compete with Someone Who Is Having Fun]] is the direct contradiction, and it is not a weak one: intrinsic enjoyment is what keeps someone in the game for ten years, and ten enjoyable years beat eighteen grim months. [[Motivation Follows Action]] adds the practical caveat — the enjoyable version is often the only one you will actually start, and a session that happens beats an optimal one that doesn't.
