# First Make It Work, Then Make It Better

## Idea
Ship the version that functions, then improve it — polishing a thing that does not yet exist is the most common way to never finish.

## Definition
The instinct is to get it right before anyone sees it, which treats quality as something applied up front. It isn't; it is something discovered through contact with reality. A working, ugly version tells you which of your worries were real, and nearly all of the ones you would have spent weeks pre-empting turn out not to be. The ordering matters more than the standard: the same person who would have shipped nothing while perfecting it will, having shipped, make it genuinely good — because now there is a specific thing to make better, and real users telling them which part.

## Source
Captured from my own reel (September 2026), built on a run of examples: the first iPhone launched without an App Store or copy-and-paste, the first cut of *Toy Story* was bad enough that Disney nearly shut production down in 1993, and Minecraft's first version was a prototype built over a weekend in May 2009 and released a week later.

The engineering formulation is Kent Beck's — "make it work, make it right, make it fast" — promoted in *Extreme Programming Explained*, though Beck has said he got it from his father, and similar phrasings appear in print as early as the 1980s. The ordering is the content: correctness before elegance, elegance before speed, and existence before any of it.

## Compass

**Roots** — *where this comes from*
This belongs to the same tradition as [[Fail Fast Fail Loudly]] — both say a problem surfaced early and loudly is cheap, while a problem avoided by not shipping is merely deferred. It rests on the stance in [[A Draft, Not a Verdict]], that a first version is raw material rather than a referendum on you.

**Paths** — *where this leads*
"Then make it better" is not a footnote; it is where [[Kaizen]] takes over and the small improvements compound on something that exists. Pacing those improvements is where [[85% Rule]] earns its keep, since the version after the first does not need to be perfect either.

**Neighbors** — *what lives nearby*
It is the shipping-shaped version of [[The Parable of the Pottery Class]], where the students graded on quantity produced the better pots. It rhymes with [[Volume Negates Luck]] and with [[Motivation Follows Action]], which locates the entire cost of a task in the minutes before starting.

**Clash** — *what pushes against this*
The real objection is that "we'll fix it later" is also how permanent messes get made — the rough version acquires users and never gets its second pass, which is the debt [[Optimize for Future Teammates Reading Your History]] is written against. And the principle is silent on judgment: knowing which roughness is acceptable and which is fatal is exactly the discrimination [[Taste Is Earned Conviction]] says you only get from having built a great many things.
