# Kaizen

## Idea
Start so small your brain can't resist — then let the momentum carry you forward.

## Definition
Kaizen (改善, "change for the better") is the Japanese philosophy of continuous improvement through tiny, consistent steps. The problem with most goals is they're too big: "work out for an hour daily," "write 2,000 words." Your brain hears the resistance and shuts down. Kaizen inverts this by making the commitment laughably small — one push-up, one sentence, one glass of water. You can't *not* do one push-up. And 90% of the time, once you start, you keep going. The one push-up becomes five, then ten, then a full workout. After 30 days of the tiny version, upgrade. The secret: you're not trying to do one push-up forever. You're making it impossible to skip the habit. Once the chain is unbroken, the behavior scales naturally.

## Source
Rooted in post-WWII Japan, influenced by W. Edwards Deming's quality control teachings. Formally popularized globally by Masaaki Imai in "Kaizen: The Key to Japan's Competitive Success" (1986). James Clear adapted the principle for personal habits in "Atomic Habits" (2018) as "the two-minute rule." Anudeep Sharma's content (YouTube, August 2026) applies it to personal goal-setting.

## Compass

**Roots** — Kaizen is a foundational principle beneath [[Consistency Over Intensity]] and [[Consistency]] — the insight that showing up daily with a tiny effort beats sporadic bursts of motivation.

**Paths** — Starting with kaizen builds the identity-based habits that [[Environment Design]] supports — once the habit is automatic, you can engineer your environment to scale it, and the compound effect connects to [[Volume Negates Luck]].

**Neighbors** — This lives near [[The Parable of the Pottery Class]] (quantity of attempts produces quality) and [[The Daily Highlight]] (protecting one small commitment each day), and complements [[Discipline Over Motivation]] by making discipline nearly effortless.

**Clash** — [[Delusional and Disciplined]] pushes back: some goals require bold, uncomfortable leaps that "one push-up" thinking can never produce — Kaizen is powerful for habit formation but insufficient for the kind of ambition that demands you be unreasonably committed from day one.
