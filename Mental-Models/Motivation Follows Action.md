# Motivation Follows Action

## Idea
The cost of any task is concentrated in the minutes before starting, so shrink the start to something trivially small and let movement generate the motivation you were waiting for.

## Definition
Twenty minutes into a workout or a writing session, you are fine. What exhausts you is the ten minutes before, when you have done nothing yet. The mistake is trying to summon the will to do the whole thing. The fix is to make starting disgustingly easy: open the page, write one line, put your shoes on. You do not have to want it; you have to be moving. Once moving, momentum supplies energy, energy produces more doing, and the loop feeds itself. Motivation is a product of action, not a prerequisite for it.

## Source
Personal reflection. The "shrink the start" tactic is James Clear's Two-Minute Rule from *Atomic Habits* (2018), which adapts David Allen's older two-minute rule from *Getting Things Done* (2001) for habit formation. The broader claim that action precedes motivation is a staple of behavioural activation in psychology.

## Compass

**Roots** — *where this comes from*
It is the practical corollary of [[Discipline Over Motivation]]: if you cannot rely on wanting to, you need a mechanism that moves you anyway, and the smallest possible first step is that mechanism.

**Paths** — *where this leads*
Once movement is the trigger, the design problem becomes [[Environment Design]], arranging the room so that the first line of code or the shoes by the door are the path of least resistance, which then compounds through [[The Flywheel Concept]].

**Neighbors** — *what lives nearby*
[[Consistency Over Intensity]] makes the same bet at a longer timescale: many small starts beat one heroic effort.

**Clash** — *what pushes against this*
Some starts should not be made easy, because [[The Exit Condition]] matters more than the entry; making it frictionless to open something you should stop doing is the same trick pointed the wrong way.
