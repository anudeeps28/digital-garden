# Creativity Is Subtraction

## Idea
When you can do anything, choosing what to leave out is the creative act, and constraints make the work better rather than smaller.

## Definition
Unlimited options paralyze. A blank page with infinite time and every tool available is harder to start than a tight brief. Kleon argues that creativity is less about adding ideas than about deciding which to leave out, and that self-imposed limits (a deadline, a word count, three colors, one tool) force inventive solutions you'd never reach with full freedom. Dr. Seuss wrote *Green Eggs and Ham* using only fifty different words, on a bet from his publisher. The skill is editing: cutting the good-but-unnecessary so what remains is clear. In an age where making is cheap, choosing what not to make is what gives the work its shape.

## Source
Austin Kleon, *[[Steal Like an Artist]]* (2012), principle #10. The lineage is long: Antoine de Saint-Exupéry's line that perfection is reached "not when there is nothing more to add, but when there is nothing left to take away" (*Wind, Sand and Stars*, 1939), and Igor Stravinsky's "the more constraints one imposes, the more one frees oneself" (*Poetics of Music*, 1942). The Green Eggs and Ham bet was with publisher Bennett Cerf (1960).

## Compass

**Roots** — *where this comes from*
It is the creative version of the [[Pareto Principle]]: most of the value sits in a few elements, so cutting the rest loses little and makes the result clearer.

**Paths** — *where this leads*
In practice it becomes [[Explicit Non-Goals]], writing down what you won't do, and [[Retention-Weighted Editing]], putting effort where it counts and cutting the rest.

**Neighbors** — *what lives nearby*
[[Slow Productivity]] applies subtraction to commitments: do fewer things so each one can be excellent. [[Taste Is Earned Conviction]] is what tells you what to cut, and taste is mostly knowing what to leave out.

**Clash** — *what pushes against this*
Early in a skill, subtraction comes too soon. [[The Parable of the Pottery Class]] and [[Volume Negates Luck]] argue you need lots of raw output before you know what's worth keeping. Cutting works on a pile of material, so you have to make the pile first.
